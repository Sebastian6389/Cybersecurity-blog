Point 1 — Social Engineering

Social engineering is a cybersecurity attack method that focuses on manipulating people rather than attacking technology directly. The attacker attempts to influence a person into revealing confidential information, granting access, transferring money, opening malicious content, or performing another action that weakens security. NIST describes social engineering as deceiving an individual into disclosing sensitive information, enabling unauthorized access, or supporting fraud by first gaining the person's confidence and trust. Because people routinely make decisions based on urgency, authority, familiarity, fear, curiosity, and helpfulness, attackers use those normal human reactions as part of the attack.

A typical social-engineering operation begins with information gathering. An attacker may study social-media profiles, company websites, staff directories, job advertisements, public documents, or previously exposed data to learn names, positions, technologies, vendors, and business routines. With that information, the attacker can build a convincing pretext, such as pretending to be a manager, help-desk technician, bank representative, delivery service, vendor, or coworker. The goal is to make the request appear normal enough that the victim reacts quickly instead of independently verifying it.

Social engineering is dangerous because technical controls can be bypassed when an authorized user is persuaded to cooperate. For example, an organization may have strong passwords, multifactor authentication, and a secure firewall, but an attacker may still succeed if an employee voluntarily provides a verification code or approves an unexpected authentication request. In this sense, the attacker is exploiting the decision-making process of the user rather than defeating the security technology itself.

Common Social-Engineering Techniques

Phishing
Fraudulent email or web content designed to make a user reveal information, visit a fake login page, or open malicious content.
Spear Phishing
A highly targeted phishing attack customized for a specific person, department, or organization.
Pretexting
The attacker creates a believable false identity or situation to persuade the victim to cooperate.
Vishing
Voice-based deception, often by telephone, in which the attacker impersonates a trusted organization or employee.
Smishing
Social engineering delivered through SMS or messaging applications, often containing urgent links or requests.
Baiting
The victim is attracted by something desirable, such as a free file, prize, download, or removable drive.
Tailgating
An unauthorized person follows an authorized employee through a secured physical entrance.
MFA Fatigue
The attacker repeatedly sends authentication requests in the hope that the victim eventually approves one.

Examples

Example 1 — Fake Help Desk: An attacker calls an employee and claims that the employee's account must be verified after a system upgrade. The attacker asks for a one-time authentication code. If the employee provides it, the attacker may be able to complete a login attempt.
Example 2 — Executive Impersonation: A finance employee receives a message that appears to come from a senior manager requesting an urgent transfer or a change to vendor payment information. The attacker depends on authority and urgency to discourage verification.
Example 3 — Physical Tailgating: An individual carrying several packages waits near a controlled entrance and asks an employee to hold the door. If the employee allows entry without confirming authorization, the attacker may gain physical access to a restricted area.

How Cybersecurity Professionals Reduce the Risk

Defending against social engineering requires both technical safeguards and consistent human procedures. Organizations can use security-awareness education, simulated phishing exercises, email filtering, password managers, multifactor authentication, least-privilege access, strong identity-verification procedures, and separate approval processes for financial or account changes. Employees should also be trained to verify unusual requests through a trusted second channel, avoid sharing passwords or one-time codes, inspect unexpected links carefully, and report suspicious interactions quickly. The most effective defense is a culture in which employees are expected to verify questionable requests instead of feeling pressured to comply immediately.

Point 1 — Ethical Hacking

Ethical hacking is authorized security testing performed to identify weaknesses before they are exploited by malicious actors. Ethical hackers use attacker-like techniques to evaluate networks, applications, systems, wireless environments, configurations, and security controls, but they operate with permission and for a defensive purpose. NIST describes penetration testing as a form of security testing in which evaluators imitate real-world attacks to identify possible ways of bypassing the security protections of an application, system, or network.

The central difference between ethical hacking and malicious hacking is not the software being used; it is the authorization, scope, intent, and handling of the results. Before a professional assessment begins, the tester and organization normally define the approved targets, permitted techniques, testing period, prohibited actions, data-handling requirements, emergency contacts, and reporting expectations. These conditions are commonly documented through a statement of work and rules of engagement. A tester who goes outside the agreed scope can create unnecessary operational risk and may no longer be acting within the authorization that makes the activity legitimate.

Ethical hacking is also broader than simply running an automated vulnerability scanner. A skilled tester interprets system behavior, confirms whether reported weaknesses are genuine, analyzes how multiple weaknesses may interact, and evaluates the potential business impact. The final objective is not merely to prove that a vulnerability exists but to provide the organization with evidence and practical remediation guidance. In a professional engagement, findings are documented carefully so system owners can prioritize corrective actions according to severity and risk.

Typical Ethical-Hacking Process

  1. Planning and authorization: establish written permission, objectives, boundaries, testing limitations, and rules of engagement.
  2. Reconnaissance: gather permitted information about the target environment, technologies, services, and exposed assets.
  3. Scanning and enumeration: identify reachable hosts, ports, services, software versions, user-facing systems, and possible weaknesses.
  4. Vulnerability analysis: examine findings to determine which weaknesses are credible, exploitable, and significant.
  5. Controlled exploitation: when specifically authorized, safely demonstrate whether a vulnerability can be used to gain unintended access or capabilities.
  6. Cleanup: remove test artifacts, temporary accounts, or files that were created during the assessment.
  7. Reporting and remediation: document evidence, severity, impact, affected assets, and recommended corrective actions.

Examples

Example 1 — Web Application Assessment: A business authorizes a penetration tester to evaluate a customer portal. The tester identifies an input-validation weakness, confirms its impact within the agreed rules, and provides developers with evidence and remediation guidance.

Example 2 — Authorized Phishing Simulation: A security team conducts an approved phishing exercise to measure whether employees recognize suspicious messages. The exercise is controlled, does not misuse real credentials, and is followed by training based on the results.

Example 3 — Wireless Security Review: An organization authorizes testing of its own wireless environment. The assessor evaluates authentication, encryption configuration, access-point exposure, segmentation, and unauthorized-access risks, then recommends configuration improvements.

Professional principle: Ethical hacking depends on explicit authorization. A security tool can be used legitimately in an approved assessment or improperly against systems for which the tester has no permission.

Point 2 — Vigenère Conference Paper Review

Selected Conference Paper

Aiman Al-Sabaawi, “Cryptanalysis of Vigenère Cipher: Method Implementation.” Presented at the 2020 IEEE Asia-Pacific Conference on Computer Science and Data Engineering (CSDE). DOI: 10.1109/CSDE50874.2020.9411383.

Open the Vigenère paper record

Review — 4 Paragraphs

My opinion of the paper's quality and position: I consider this paper a useful academic introduction to the practical cryptanalysis of the Vigenère cipher because it moves beyond describing how the cipher works and focuses on how its weaknesses can be analyzed. The implementation-centered approach is valuable for cybersecurity students because it demonstrates that an encryption system can appear complex while still leaking patterns that make systematic analysis possible. In my view, the paper is strongest as an instructional resource rather than as evidence for a modern cryptographic technique, because Vigenère is historically important but no longer suitable for protecting contemporary systems.

Did the writer do sufficient research? For the limited objective of demonstrating Vigenère cryptanalysis, I believe the author provides a sufficient foundation. The paper connects cryptanalysis with the examination of available ciphertext information and applies that idea directly to the Vigenère cipher. This makes the research direction clear and gives the reader a practical connection between classical cryptographic theory and attack methodology. However, I would have preferred a broader experimental section showing how consistently the method succeeds across different plaintext lengths, key lengths, and text samples.

What could have been enhanced? The argument could be strengthened by presenting more quantitative evidence. For example, the paper could compare Kasiski examination, Index of Coincidence, and other statistical methods using the same ciphertext samples and then report success rates, processing time, and failure cases. More discussion of the conditions under which the attack becomes unreliable would also improve the paper because readers could better understand both the strengths and limitations of the proposed implementation. That additional evidence would make the conclusions more persuasive and easier to reproduce.

What should a follow-up article contain, and did I agree with the paper? A useful follow-up study should use a larger and clearly documented dataset, publish reproducible code, test several key lengths and message sizes, and compare multiple cryptanalytic strategies under identical conditions. It could also explain how lessons from Vigenère cryptanalysis relate to broader principles such as key reuse and statistical leakage while distinguishing classical ciphers from modern standards. I agree with the paper's educational value because studying how Vigenère can be broken illustrates why historical encryption mechanisms should not be considered secure today. The article supported rather than changed my opinion: classical ciphers remain valuable teaching tools, but modern information protection requires substantially stronger cryptographic designs.

Point 2 — Playfair Conference Paper Review

Selected Conference Paper

Yuzhe Wang, “A Classical Cipher—Playfair Cipher and Its Improved Versions.” Published in the proceedings of the 2021 International Conference on Electronic Information Engineering and Computer Science (EIECS), pp. 123–126. DOI: 10.1109/EIECS53707.2021.9587989.

Open the Playfair paper record

Review — 4 Paragraphs

My opinion of the paper's quality and position: I found this paper effective as a concise review of the Playfair cipher and several attempts to extend it. The author places the classical technique within substitution cryptography and discusses three improved versions of the 3D Playfair cipher. That comparison is useful because it shows how researchers can modify an older design by changing its structure and symbol arrangement in an effort to increase complexity. I view the paper primarily as a survey and educational discussion rather than as proof that a modified Playfair system should replace modern cryptographic algorithms.

Did the writer do sufficient research? For a short conference paper, I think the writer performed enough research to provide a meaningful overview of the traditional cipher and the improved variants being discussed. The paper does more than explain the original method; it compares several modifications and considers their development. That gives the reader useful context for understanding how classical cryptographic ideas evolve. At the same time, the short length limits the amount of evidence available, so the review would be stronger if more implementation results were included.

What could have been enhanced? The paper would benefit from a deeper technical comparison of the improved versions. I would like to see measurements involving keyspace size, processing time, resistance to frequency-based analysis, memory requirements, behavior on different message lengths, and identifiable weaknesses in each design. It would also help to separate increases in complexity from genuine increases in security. A cipher can become more complicated without necessarily reaching the security level expected from a modern, extensively analyzed cryptographic standard.

What should a follow-up article contain, and did I agree with the paper? A strong follow-up article should implement each reviewed Playfair variant under the same conditions, publish the source code and test data, and compare the results using consistent security and performance criteria. It could also use a modern algorithm such as AES as a reference point so readers can distinguish the academic value of classical-cipher modifications from production-grade encryption. I agree with the article's value as a study of how Playfair has been modified, but I do not interpret those improvements as making Playfair appropriate for modern sensitive data. The article reinforced my existing position that Playfair is most useful for learning the development of cryptographic ideas and evaluating how design changes affect a cipher.
